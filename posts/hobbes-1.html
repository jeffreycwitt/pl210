The Leviathan, Introduction and cc. 5-7

Consider how "nature" and "art" are described and related in the opening lines.
When he says "nature" what does he mean?
How is art related to nature? How is it similar to nature or different?
Why is the Leviathan or the State said to be a product of art? How is it an imitation of nature?
As the State is described, what kind of picture of the human person emerges?
At the bottom of 117b Hobbes gives a description of his plan for the rest of his book. What does he plan to do?
Regarding the first part of his investigation, he criticizes where others have begun their investigation and instructs us to start elsewhere. Where does he think we should begin?
Does this tell us anything about his procedure?
Is there something distinctively modern about this starting point?
Could he be rejecting the methods of the past in some way? If so, how?

While I'm not asking you to read chapters 1-4, it is a good idea to look at the titles of these chapters and browse around to get a sense of what Hobbes is up to.

What definition does Hobbes give to the word "reason" (c. 5, p. 128b)?
Why does Hobbes think Philosophers have so often been led into error? What is needed to correct this? (c. 5, p. 130)
On p. 131a, Hobbes gives a nice summary of how the mind thinks, how it ensures clarity, and how it proceeds to the "benefit of mankind". What sorts of things are said to prevent us from this clarity and leave humanity without benefit?
In chapter 6, pay particular attention to what Hobbes defines as "good" (p. 132b).
The idea of "fear" becomes important later on in the text, so make sure to identify the definition Hobbes assigns to "Fear" (see p. 133).
From chapter 7, be able to distinguish the difference between Opinion, Science, and Belief or Faith.
In whom do we really trust, when we have faith in religious doctrine?
Why does Hobbes show this early concern for religion? Could there be any historical reasons for this concern (Hobbes lived from 1588-1679)?
What's your reaction to Hobbes's definition of reason? Does it seem different than other conceptions of reason that you've seen in other thinkers? How so?
Does Hobbes's understanding of the "good" seem correct or problematic to you? Is it different from ideas of the good seen in other philosophers?
